# Sales Page Architect

Write a complete, section-by-section sales page — not an outline, not a
template with blanks. The page must be ready to paste into a page builder
and publish. Every claim must be either backed by supplied proof or
explicitly flagged as unproven.

This skill runs the user's inputs through eight expert lenses in a fixed
order. Each step produces an artifact that feeds the next one. Do not skip
steps or write the page before completing steps 1–5 — the page is an output
of the analysis, not a replacement for it.

Load `reference/experts.md` for the specific framework each expert
contributes and what their lens checks for. Load `reference/template.md`
for the section-by-section page skeleton and the exact format of the
final deliverable.

## 1. Intake

Before starting, make sure you have:

- **The offer** — what exactly is being sold (product/service, what's
  included, format, delivery).
- **The price** — and any payment structure (one-time, payment plan,
  subscription).
- **The buyer** — who they are, what they're struggling with, what they've
  already tried.
- **Proof** — testimonials, case studies, results, credentials, data,
  guarantees, media mentions — whatever exists. If the user says "I don't
  have any," proceed but flag every claim that needs proof in the final
  audit instead of inventing it.
- **Optional: an existing page** — if supplied, this is a rewrite job. Run
  the diagnostic in step 0a before drafting anything new.

If any required input is missing, ask for it directly rather than
guessing or inventing buyer pain points, prices, or results. Fabricated
specifics are worse than asking one more question.

### 0a. Diagnostic mode (rewrite only)

If the user pastes an existing page, diagnose it against the same five
lenses before touching the copy:

1. What awareness stage is the current copy written for, and does it match
   the actual buyer (step 1)?
2. Which claims are specific and provable vs. vague filler (step 2)?
3. Is the headline doing real mechanical work, or is it generic (step 3)?
4. Is there a value stack, or is the price just stated with no
   justification (step 4)?
5. Does the page address objections before they're raised, or does it
   hope the reader doesn't think of them (step 5)?

Present this diagnosis as a short bulleted list before the rewritten page,
so the user sees what changed and why.

## 2. Awareness diagnosis (Eugene Schwartz)

Identify which of the five market awareness stages the buyer is in:

1. **Unaware** — doesn't know they have the problem.
2. **Problem-aware** — knows the problem, doesn't know solutions exist.
3. **Solution-aware** — knows solutions exist, hasn't seen this one.
4. **Product-aware** — knows this product, hasn't decided to buy.
5. **Most aware** — knows the product, just needs the deal/reason to act now.

State the stage and why, in 1–3 sentences, using specifics from the buyer
description. This determines the lead: unaware/problem-aware buyers need a
story or agitation lead; product-aware/most-aware buyers can take a direct
offer or mechanism lead. Carry this decision into the headline step and the
page draft — do not write a "most aware" direct-offer headline for an
unaware audience.

## 3. Claims extraction & specificity audit (Claude Hopkins)

List every factual claim that could go on the page. For each one mark it:

- **Specific & provable** — a number, a name, a mechanism, a timeframe.
- **Vague** — "amazing results," "the best," "life-changing" — flag these
  and either sharpen them using the supplied proof or drop them. Hopkins'
  rule: a specific claim ("3,427 students," "in 14 days," "a 41-point
  drop") outperforms a superlative every time, and only specific claims
  survive the proof audit in step 8.

Output this as a short table: Claim → Specific or Vague → Sharpened
version (or "needs real data").

## 4. Headline generation (John Caples)

Generate 12 headlines across 6 different mechanics, 2 per mechanic:

1. **Self-interest** — direct benefit to the reader.
2. **Curiosity** — open a gap the reader needs closed.
3. **News** — what's new/different now.
4. **How-to** — promises a method.
5. **Question** — engages the reader's own situation.
6. **Proof/testimonial** — leads with evidence or a result.

After listing all 12, pick one as the lead headline and state why in 1–2
sentences, tying the choice back to the awareness stage from step 2 (e.g.
a most-aware buyer responds to a proof headline; an unaware buyer needs
curiosity or news to even stop scrolling).

## 5. Value stack & price justification (Alex Hormozi / Russell Brunson)

Build the value stack:

- List every component of the offer with an honest standalone value
  estimate (what it would cost or sell for alone).
- Sum to a total perceived value.
- State the actual price.
- Calculate the perceived-value-to-price ratio (total value ÷ price).
- Note anything that increases perceived likelihood of success or reduces
  time-to-result or effort — these matter as much as the line items
  (Hormozi's value equation: dream outcome × perceived likelihood, divided
  by time delay × effort).

## 6. False beliefs breakdown (Hormozi's belief gap)

Identify the three false beliefs most likely blocking this specific buyer
*before the offer is even named*. These are usually some mix of:

- **Internal belief** — "I'm not the kind of person who can do this."
- **Vehicle belief** — "This type of solution doesn't work / isn't for me."
- **External belief** — "Forces outside my control (time, money, market)
  will stop this from working."

For each of the three, write one sentence naming the belief and one
sentence on how the page will dismantle it (a story, a mechanism
explanation, a proof point, a reframe). These three beliefs must each be
addressed somewhere in the drafted page — note where.

## 7. Draft the page (Kennedy, Brunson, Halbert, Bencivenga)

Now write the full page, section by section, using `reference/template.md`
as the skeleton. Pull from all four remaining experts as you write:

- **Dan Kennedy** — direct, urgent, no fluff; always a clear single call
  to action; deadlines/scarcity must be real, never fake.
- **Russell Brunson** — the offer is revealed as the resolution to a story
  and a stack, not just listed; "attractive character" framing where a
  founder/origin story fits.
- **Gary Halbert** — write to one specific reader, conversationally; the
  lead must hook in the first two sentences; agitate the problem before
  presenting the mechanism.
- **Gary Bencivenga** — every claim needs a "because" — a reason, proof,
  or mechanism that makes it believable, not just assertable. Bencivenga's
  test: a skeptical reader asks "why should I believe that?" after every
  sentence — the next sentence should answer it.

The page must be complete prose, ready to publish — full sentences in
every section, not bullet placeholders or "[insert testimonial here]". If
proof for a section doesn't exist, write the section honestly without
inventing fake specifics, and flag the gap in the step 8 audit instead.

## 8. Proof audit

After the draft, produce a table of every major claim made on the page:

| Claim | Proof used | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| ... | testimonial / data / guarantee / mechanism / none supplied | Strong / Moderate / Needs evidence |

Any row marked "Needs evidence" is a flag for the user, not a reason to
have invented something. Close with 2–3 concrete suggestions for what
proof to gather next to strengthen the weakest claims.

## Output

Deliver, in this order:
1. (Rewrite jobs only) the diagnostic from step 0a.
2. The full publish-ready page (step 7).
3. A compact "Strategy Behind The Page" appendix covering steps 2–6 and
   the proof audit (step 8), so the user can see and reuse the thinking,
   not just the copy.
